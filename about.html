What I work on

The common thread across everything on this site is inference at the level of the field rather than the summary statistic, and uncertainty that is worth quoting. A component-separated CMB map used as if it were the true CMB throws away the uncertainty that separation introduced. A stacked spectrum reported without a bootstrap says nothing about whether the feature is real. Most of my effort goes into the part after a method appears to work — building the evaluation that could falsify it, and reporting what that evaluation returns.

Concretely: conditional diffusion models for probabilistic CMB component separation; wavelet-domain approaches to galaxy survey analysis and redshift-space distortions; stacking and cross-matching frameworks for wide-field spectroscopic and photometric surveys; and modelling of high-redshift supermassive black hole growth.

How I work

Everything here was built on a shared HPC cluster without a large research group behind it, which has shaped the habits more than any methodological preference has.

  • Design the falsifying test before believing the result. The open-loop PySM3 evaluation on the CMB project exists because a closed-loop metric could not have caught what it caught. It reversed the ranking of three models.
  • Report nulls at the same resolution as detections. The SPHEREx work is a non-detection. It has calibrated upper limits, a documented selection effect, and a pilot signal that failed to replicate — all of which are stated plainly rather than buried.
  • Write down what broke. A NaN cascade caused by a loss term two orders of magnitude out of scale, and a preprocessing bug that made an evaluation circular at r = 0.996, both appear in the CMB write-up. They cost weeks and they are the most transferable thing in the project.
  • Ablate before explaining. When η* came out large, the interesting question was not how to reduce it but what it was a property of. Four loss configurations returning the same value answered that.

Tooling is PyTorch, healpy, astropy and SLURM on Penn State's ICDS Roar Collab cluster, with enough numerical debugging to have opinions about gradient clipping.
